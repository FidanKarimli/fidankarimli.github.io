I learned about graphs at the PSI:ML summer school, and I loved the idea straight away. This is a short note on what I understood, written for someone starting from zero. It is the background for my [protein stability post](/posts/jepa-for-proteins/), so I'll keep it light.

## What is a graph?

A graph is a set of **nodes** and the **edges** that connect them. That's all.

Friends on a social network are a graph: people are nodes, friendships are edges. A molecule is a graph: atoms are nodes, bonds are edges. A map is a graph too: cities and the roads between them.

What matters is that the data is about <Highlight text="things and how they are connected" client:visible />. An image is a neat grid and a sentence is a neat line, but a molecule is neither. Squeezing it into a grid or a line throws away the part that matters, which is who is connected to whom. Each node can carry numbers (an atom's type, a person's age), and so can each edge (a bond length, how long two people have been friends).

## Graphs are not new

It is easy to think graphs arrived with deep learning. They didn't. <Highlight text="They have been the natural form of connected data for almost 300 years." client:visible />

- **1736.** Euler solves the Seven Bridges of Königsberg puzzle by thinking only about which landmasses are joined by bridges. It is usually called the first theorem of graph theory ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/science/graph-theory)).
- **The 19th century.** The word "graph" is credited to James Sylvester, who used it for diagrams of molecules, so chemistry and graphs have been linked from the start.
- **1936.** Dénes Kőnig publishes the first textbook on the subject ([Biggs, Lloyd and Wilson](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/Graph_Theory,_1736%E2%80%931936) tell this history).
- **Late 1990s.** Google's PageRank ranks web pages by treating the web as a graph of links.
- **2009.** Scarselli et al. propose the first model called a graph neural network ([paper](https://papers.baulab.info/papers/Scarselli-2009.pdf)).
- **2017.** Gilmer et al. frame it as message passing, and Kipf and Welling publish the graph convolutional network. Modern GNNs start here.

So the new part is not graphs. It is that we can now <Highlight text="learn from them with neural networks" client:visible />.

## Most recommender systems are graphs

Think of Netflix, YouTube or an online shop. Users and items are nodes. A click, a purchase or a rating is an edge. Add friendships between users, and facts about the items (brand, colour, category), and you get a big graph with several kinds of edges, like the picture below, where each person is a node and each dotted line is a connection.

Predicting what a user will like becomes a question about the graph: is there an edge missing between this user and that item? That is why most modern recommenders use graph methods. Pinterest runs a graph network (PinSage) on a graph with 3 billion nodes and 18 billion edges, and recent surveys describe GNNs as the new state of the art for recommendation ([Wu et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.02260); [Gao et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.12843)). It is also the area I do research in at Istanbul Technical University.

## What a graph neural network does

A graph neural network (GNN) learns a vector for every node, a short list of numbers that describes the node and its surroundings. It does this by repeating one simple idea: <Highlight text="ask your neighbours" client:visible />.

In one layer:

1. Every node sends its current vector to its neighbours. These are the <HoverCard label="messages" title="Message passing" body="Each node sends its vector, together with any edge information, to the nodes it is connected to. The name comes from Gilmer et al. (2017)." client:visible />.
2. Every node adds up the messages it receives. The sum doesn't depend on the order, which matters because graphs have no natural order.
3. Every node updates its own vector from what it received and what it already knew, using a small neural network.

Then you stack layers. After one layer a node knows about its direct neighbours. After two, about its neighbours' neighbours, and so on. The number of layers decides how far a node can "see".

<HandHops client:visible />

That's most of it. Different GNNs (GCN, GAT, GIN and others) mostly differ in how they do steps 2 and 3.

## What can you predict?

Once every node has a vector, you can ask three kinds of questions:

- **About a node:** what kind of user is this? Which atom is this?
- **About an edge:** will this user like this item? Do these two residues touch?
- **About the whole graph:** is this molecule toxic? You combine all node vectors into one and predict from it.

## Why this matters for proteins

A protein is a chain of amino acids, but it folds into a 3D shape, and residues far apart in the chain can end up right next to each other. If we make each residue a node and join the ones that touch, the graph captures exactly those contacts. A GNN can then learn how a residue feels about its surroundings, and that is what decides whether a mutation hurts.

That is the setup in my [protein stability post](/posts/jepa-for-proteins/).

## Two things that go wrong

- **Too many layers.** If you keep passing messages, every node starts to look like every other node, and the useful differences blur. This is called over-smoothing. Most GNNs stay shallow for this reason (mine has four layers).
- **Far-away information.** A node can only hear from nodes within as many hops as it has layers. Long-range effects need more layers or another trick.

## Where to read more

<div class="credits">

- [A Gentle Introduction to Graph Neural Networks](https://distill.pub/2021/gnn-intro/) by Sanchez-Lengeling et al. (Distill, 2021). Interactive, and the best place to start.
- [Stanford CS224W: Machine Learning with Graphs](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs224w/) by Jure Leskovec. A full course with free slides and lectures.
- [The Graph Neural Network Model](https://papers.baulab.info/papers/Scarselli-2009.pdf) by Scarselli et al. (2009). Where the name comes from.
- [Neural Message Passing for Quantum Chemistry](https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.01212) by Gilmer et al. (2017). The paper that framed GNN layers as message passing.
- [Semi-Supervised Classification with Graph Convolutional Networks](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.02907) by Kipf and Welling (2017). The classic GCN paper.
- [How Powerful are Graph Neural Networks?](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.00826) by Xu et al. (2019). Why the sum in step 2 is a good choice (GIN).
- [Graph Convolutional Neural Networks for Web-Scale Recommender Systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.01973) by Ying et al. (2018). PinSage at Pinterest.
- [Graph Neural Networks in Recommender Systems: A Survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.02260) by Wu et al. A good overview of the field.
- [Graph Neural Network at Google](https://indico.cern.ch/event/1496673/contributions/6657140/attachments/3129599/5551779/%5BEXTERNAL%5D%20Graph%20Neural%20Network%20at%20Google%20--%20Fast%20Machine%20Learning%20for%20Science,%202025.pdf) by Mathieu Guillame-Bert (Fast Machine Learning for Science, 2025). Slides on how GNNs are used and scaled in practice.
- [A practical introduction to GNNs](https://danielegrattarola.github.io/posts/2021-03-12/gnn-lecture-part-2.html) by Daniele Grattarola. Lecture notes with code.
